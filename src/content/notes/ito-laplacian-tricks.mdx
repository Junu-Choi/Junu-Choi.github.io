## Setup

Consider the Itô stochastic differential equation
$$
dX_t = b(X_t)\, dt + \sigma(X_t)\, dW_t
$$
on $\mathbb{R}^d$, where $b: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d$ is the drift, $\sigma: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ is the diffusion matrix, and $W_t$ is a $d$-dimensional Brownian motion. For a sufficiently smooth test function $f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$, **Itô's lemma** (Kuo, 2006, ch. 7) reads
$$
df(X_t) \;=\; \Big[\, \langle b,\, \nabla f \rangle \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\sigma \sigma^\top \nabla^{2} f\big) \,\Big] dt \;+\; \langle \nabla f,\, \sigma\, dW_t \rangle.
$$
The infinitesimal generator $\mathcal{L}$ is therefore
$$
\mathcal{L} f \;=\; \langle b, \nabla f \rangle \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, \operatorname{tr}\!\big(\sigma \sigma^\top \nabla^{2} f\big),
$$
The second term — half the trace of the diffusion-weighted Hessian — is the **second-order part of the generator**. With $a(x, t) := \sigma(x, t) \sigma(x, t)^\top$ writing it as $\tfrac{1}{2}\, a^{ij}\, \partial^{2}_{ij} f$ identifies it as a *generalized* (Hörmander-type) Laplacian; it reduces to the standard $\tfrac{1}{2} \Delta f$ only in the isotropic case $\sigma \equiv I$. For brevity, I will refer to it below as **the trace term**.

This term is everywhere in SDE-based machine learning. It appears in the Fokker-Planck equation that governs the marginal density $p_t$ of $X_t$, in the continuous-time change-of-variables formula used by continuous normalising flows, and in the implicit score-matching objective once one integrates by parts. Whenever a learning algorithm asks for $\mathcal{L} f$ where $f$ is a neural network with $d$-dimensional input, the trace term becomes the dominant cost: $\nabla^{2} f$ is a $d \times d$ Hessian, and computing its trace exactly requires $O(d)$ Hessian-vector products, i.e. $O(d)$ backward passes. For $d$ in the thousands — image pixels, dense embeddings, particle states — this is prohibitive.

In a strict sense, there is **only one general-purpose method** for handling this term: Hutchinson's stochastic estimator. It is the only response that preserves the SDE you actually care about. The two other responses commonly invoked in the literature — isotropic $\sigma$ and Langevin sampling — are not methods at all in this sense; they are **bypasses** that work by changing the problem. The first changes the *SDE itself* (and only applies when the diffusion was a design choice in the first place); the second drops trace estimation in favour of sampling and only applies when sampling is what you ultimately wanted. Both are widely used, both are reasonable in their settings, but conflating them with Hutchinson elides the question *whose problem are you solving?*

## The method · Hutchinson's stochastic trace estimator

The method is to replace the deterministic trace with an unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate.

**Lemma (Hutchinson, 1990).** For any square matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$ and any random vector $\xi \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ with $\mathbb{E}[\xi] = 0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\xi \xi^\top] = I$,
$$
\operatorname{tr}(A) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{\xi}\!\left[\, \xi^\top A\, \xi \,\right].
$$

Applied to the Hessian, this gives the estimator
$$
\widehat{\operatorname{tr}}_N(\nabla^{2} f) \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \xi_{n}^{\top}\, \nabla^{2} f \, \xi_{n}.
$$
Each quadratic form $\xi^\top \nabla^{2} f\, \xi$ requires **one Hessian-vector product**, evaluated as
$$
\xi^\top \nabla^{2} f\, \xi \;=\; \xi^\top\, \nabla_x \langle \nabla f,\, \xi \rangle,
$$
i.e. a forward pass through $f$, a backward pass for $\nabla f$, an inner product with $\xi$, and a second backward pass — $O(1)$ in the input dimension $d$. The original $O(d)$ trace becomes $O(N)$ in the number of probes.

**Choice of probe distribution.** Among iid distributions with $\mathbb{E}[\xi_i] = 0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\xi_i) = 1$, Rademacher entries ($\xi_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ with equal probability) minimise the variance of $\xi^\top A\, \xi$ for symmetric $A$ (Hutchinson, 1990; Roosta-Khorasani & Ascher, 2015). Expanding $\xi^\top A\, \xi = \sum_i A_{ii}\, \xi_i^{2} + 2 \sum_{i < j} A_{ij}\, \xi_i \xi_j$, the Rademacher condition $\xi_i^{2} \equiv 1$ makes the **diagonal contribution exact** ($\sum_i A_{ii} = \operatorname{tr}(A)$), so all Monte-Carlo variance comes from the off-diagonal cross terms; with $A = \nabla^2 f$, a Hessian whose diagonal carries most of the signal, this is a real saving. Gaussian probes have a strictly larger variance — by $2 \sum_i A_{ii}^{2}$ — but are convenient when one needs differentiability in $\xi$.

**Where this estimator powers things.** FFJORD (Grathwohl et al., 2019) trained continuous normalising flows at image-scale dimension precisely because Hutchinson's trick lets one estimate the log-determinant trace appearing in the continuous change-of-variables formula. The same estimator underlies sliced score matching (Song et al., 2020) and a number of Hessian-spectrum analyses of neural networks (e.g., Yao et al., 2020).

The price is variance: $\widehat{\operatorname{tr}}_N$ is unbiased but its standard deviation falls only as $N^{-1/2}$, and large rank-1 components of $A$ inflate the constant. This is why the next two tricks are still useful when one can afford a structural assumption.

## Bypass 1 · A different SDE: constrain $\sigma$ to isotropic

The first bypass is not a method at all in the sense of the previous section. Rather than computing the trace on the SDE you started with, it asks you to **commit to a different SDE in the first place** — one whose diffusion $\sigma(x, t) = \sigma(t)\, I$ is spatially isotropic — and then exploits the special structure of that SDE to make the trace disappear from the training objective entirely.

This is only available if the diffusion was your design choice to begin with. If the SDE is given to you by physics, geometry, or data covariance — for example, a manifold-aware diffusion that respects a local metric, or coloured noise inferred from a covariance model — this bypass does not apply, and Hutchinson is the only recourse.

If $\sigma(x, t) = \sigma(t)\, I$ — time-dependent but **spatially isotropic** diffusion — then
$$
\sigma \sigma^\top \;=\; \sigma^2(t)\, I, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(\sigma \sigma^\top \nabla^{2} f) \;=\; \sigma^{2}(t)\, \Delta f,
$$
where $\Delta f = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \partial_{i}^{2} f$ is the ordinary Laplacian. This is still $O(d)$ to evaluate exactly, but the scalar form admits a sharper simplification: a change-of-target identity removes the Hessian-trace from the *training* objective entirely.

Recall Hyvärinen's (2005) implicit score-matching loss for a learned score $s_\theta \approx \nabla \log p$,
$$
J_{\mathrm{ISM}}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{p(x)} \!\left[\, \tfrac{1}{2}\, \lVert s_\theta(x) \rVert^2 \;+\; \nabla \!\cdot\! s_\theta(x) \,\right] \;+\; C,
$$
obtained from the explicit Fisher-divergence loss by an integration by parts; the divergence $\nabla \!\cdot\! s_\theta = \operatorname{tr}(\nabla s_\theta)$ is structurally the same trace problem as before. **Vincent's (2011) denoising score-matching identity** then re-routes back to a Fisher-divergence form against a noise-perturbed density: for an isotropic-Gaussian corruption $\tilde{x} = x + \sigma \epsilon$ with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, one has
$$
J_{\mathrm{DSM}}(\theta) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, \mathbb{E}_{x, \epsilon} \!\left[\, \big\lVert s_\theta(\tilde{x}) + \tfrac{\epsilon}{\sigma} \big\rVert^{2} \,\right] \;\equiv\; J_{\mathrm{ISM}}(\theta) \text{ (under }p_\sigma\text{), up to constants}.
$$
The right-hand side is a pure regression target: no Hessian, no trace.

The variance-exploding and variance-preserving SDEs of Song et al. (2021),
$$
dX_t \;=\; \sqrt{\tfrac{d}{dt} \sigma^{2}(t)}\, dW_t \qquad \text{and} \qquad dX_t \;=\; -\tfrac{1}{2}\, \beta(t)\, X_t\, dt \;+\; \sqrt{\beta(t)}\, dW_t,
$$
both belong to this isotropic class, which is why DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) and the score-based SDE framework can train at image-scale dimension without ever materialising $\nabla^{2} f$.

**The cost.** Isotropy is a genuine modelling assumption. Geometric or manifold-aware diffusion — where the noise should respect a local metric — cannot be expressed as $\sigma(t)\, I$. In those settings one either generalises the denoising-score-matching identity (cf. De Bortoli et al., 2022 for diffusion on Riemannian manifolds) or falls back to Hutchinson.

## Bypass 2 · A different question: Langevin sampling

The second bypass is even further from a "method". It does not compute the trace; it does not even ask for its value. Instead, it **changes the question** from "estimate $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma\sigma^\top \nabla^2 f)$ on this SDE" to "sample from a target $\pi$ via a stochastic process whose stationary distribution is $\pi$". The Laplacian is then absorbed by the white-noise injection of that process; it is never computed because the algorithm never asks for it.

This is only available if the downstream task is itself sampling. If you wanted a density value, a log-likelihood, or any quantity that exposes the Laplacian as a number, this bypass does not apply.

Consider sampling from a density $\pi(x) \propto \exp(-U(x))$. The overdamped Langevin SDE
$$
dX_t \;=\; -\nabla U(X_t)\, dt \;+\; \sqrt{2}\, dW_t
$$
has $\pi$ as its unique stationary distribution under mild conditions (Roberts & Tweedie, 1996). The associated Fokker-Planck equation is
$$
\partial_{t}\, p_t \;=\; \nabla \cdot (p_t\, \nabla U) \;+\; \Delta p_t,
$$
and crucially the Laplacian term $\Delta p_t$ — which would be expensive to evaluate if we tried — is **implicit in the white-noise injection** $\sqrt{2}\, dW_t$. Euler-Maruyama discretisation gives the unadjusted Langevin algorithm; replacing the exact $\nabla U$ with a stochastic-gradient estimate gives Welling & Teh's (2011) SGLD. In both cases the Laplacian never appears in code.

This is more than a sampling trick. Under Anderson's (1982) time-reversal identity, the reverse-time SDE associated with a forward $dX_t = b\, dt + \sigma\, dW_t$ is
$$
dX_t \;=\; \big[\, b(X_t, t) \;-\; \nabla \!\cdot\! (\sigma \sigma^\top)(X_t, t) \;-\; \sigma \sigma^\top(X_t, t)\, \nabla_{x} \log p_t(X_t) \,\big]\, dt \;+\; \sigma(X_t, t)\, d\bar{W}_t,
$$
where the divergence term $\nabla \!\cdot\! (\sigma\sigma^\top)$ has $j$-th component $\sum_i \partial_i (\sigma \sigma^\top)_{ij}$. **For spatially-constant diffusion** — in particular $\sigma(x, t) = \sigma(t)\, I$ as in Bypass 1 — that divergence vanishes and the expression collapses to
$$
dX_t \;=\; \big[\, b(X_t, t) \;-\; \sigma^{2}(t)\, \nabla_{x} \log p_t(X_t) \,\big]\, dt \;+\; \sigma(t)\, d\bar{W}_t,
$$
which is the form one sees in Song et al. (2021). This reverse SDE is a Langevin-type system whose drift is exactly the (learned) score and whose noise carries the second-order term of the running generator. Off-the-shelf SDE solvers — Euler-Maruyama, Heun, the higher-order schemes of Karras et al. (2022) — integrate this without ever differentiating through $\nabla^{2}$. The trace was paid for once, implicitly, in the choice of noise schedule.

The connection between the two bypasses is then exact: **isotropy makes the score-matching loss tractable; Langevin sampling makes the resulting score-based model usable**. Together they explain why diffusion-style generative models scaled to image and audio dimension without anyone writing a Hessian-vector product.

The cost is Monte-Carlo variance and discretisation error. Total wall-clock depends on how many integration steps are required, which depends on how well the noise schedule is tuned — a more or less separate research problem.

## Experiments

Of the three responses, only the **method** (Hutchinson) produces a numerical estimate of the trace on an arbitrary SDE; the bypasses produce a different object. The experiment is therefore built around comparing Hutchinson against an exact baseline, with the bypasses illustrated separately.

**Hardware.** All runs on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell (96 GiB, CUDA 13.2) with PyTorch 2.11. Test function: a 3-layer MLP $f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}$ with hidden width 256 and tanh activations, $x \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$. Exact trace via $d$ HVPs against canonical basis vectors; Hutchinson via the double-backprop identity from the previous section.

A practical caveat the theory section understates: for this MLP the two probe distributions are **practically indistinguishable**. The theoretical advantage of Rademacher — variance smaller by $2 \sum_i A_{ii}^{2}$ — is real, but for a generic neural-network Hessian at random input the diagonal contribution is small relative to the Frobenius mass on the off-diagonal, and the gap closes empirically. At $d = 100$ with the true trace at $\approx 0.034$, roughly $N = 100$ probes brings the standard deviation below $1\%$.

## Tradeoffs

|                                          | what it does                            | preserves the SDE? | gives a trace value? | when it applies                            |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------ | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| **Method** · Hutchinson                  | unbiased stochastic estimate            | yes                | yes (with MC noise)  | any $\sigma$; you need an actual number    |
| **Bypass 1** · Isotropic $\sigma(t)\, I$ | trace removed from the loss via DSM     | no — by design     | no                   | $\sigma$ is yours to choose                |
| **Bypass 2** · Langevin sampling         | trace implicit in white noise           | yes (but reverse-time form requires isotropy) | no                   | the downstream task is sampling, not estimation |
